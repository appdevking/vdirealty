State guide

Closing costs by state: WA, AZ, NY

Typical buyer and seller costs in VDI Realty's three farm states — with agent compensation separated from taxes, title, escrow, lender and attorney charges so the ranges do not hide the biggest variable.

Read this first

Agent compensation is not baked into the first table

Compensation is negotiable and depends on the listing agreement, buyer agreement and final contract. The planning ranges below first exclude all agent compensation. A second table then shows what seller totals look like if the seller's agreement makes the seller responsible for total compensation modeled at 5%–6% of price.

Cash-to-close is not the same as closing costs. Down payment, prepaid property taxes and insurance, initial escrow deposits and prepaid interest affect the cash a buyer needs, but they are not all fees. The ranges here focus on transaction charges and taxes; ask the lender for a Loan Estimate that separates them.

Washington

Washington: REET drives the seller side

Typical buyer items

Loan origination and underwriting, appraisal, credit report, lender's title policy, escrow share, recording, flood certification, and prepaid taxes/insurance shown separately by the lender.

Typical seller items

Real estate excise tax (REET), owner's title policy where customary or negotiated, escrow share, deed recording/release charges, payoff-related fees and prorations.

For 2026, Washington's state REET is graduated: 1.10% on the portion up to $525,000; 1.28% on the portion to $1,525,000; 2.75% on the portion to $3,025,000; and 3.00% above that. Counties and cities can add local REET, and thresholds are periodically adjusted — confirm the current Washington Department of Revenue schedule for your closing date.

Arizona

Arizona: no state transfer tax

Typical buyer items

Loan charges, appraisal, credit report, lender's title policy, escrow share, recording, inspections, and prepaid taxes/insurance shown separately by the lender.

Typical seller items

Owner's title policy where required by the contract or local custom, escrow share, recording/release charges, HOA disclosure and transfer charges, payoff fees and prorations.

Arizona's lack of a transfer tax is the main reason its non-compensation seller range is lower than Washington's or New York's. HOA communities can narrow that advantage when disclosure, transfer and prepaid association charges stack up.

New York

New York: attorneys and layered taxes

Typical buyer items

Buyer's attorney, lender and bank-attorney charges, appraisal, title insurance for houses/condos, recording, mortgage recording tax where applicable, and the mansion tax on residential purchases of $1 million or more.

Typical seller items

Seller's attorney, New York State transfer tax, title/closing adjustments, recording and payoff items, and any building flip tax or association charge imposed by the property's documents.

  • State transfer tax: generally $2 per $500 of consideration — 0.4% — and normally paid by the seller unless the contract shifts it.
  • Mansion tax: an additional buyer tax beginning at 1% for residential consideration of $1 million or more; higher NYC supplemental rates apply at higher price tiers.
  • NYC transfer tax: for common residential resales, the city tax is generally 1% at $500,000 or less and 1.425% above $500,000, normally seller-paid by custom or contract.
  • Mortgage recording tax: many NYC residential mortgages fall around 1.8%–1.925% of the loan before property-specific rules and credits; this is a major reason financed NYC purchases cost more to close.
  • Co-ops are different: a share loan is not a mortgage on real property, so mortgage recording tax and standard title treatment differ; board, bank and building fees take their place.
Cross-check

Averages are a sanity check, not a quote

Bankrate's state closing-cost study, using LodeStar data, reported average purchase closing costs including taxes of about $3,574 in Arizona, $5,995 in Washington and $13,738 in New York. Those are averages at each state's own average sale price and mix of transactions — useful for spotting an estimate that is wildly off, not for applying one percentage to every home.
